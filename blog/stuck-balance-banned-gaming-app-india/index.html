Money Stuck in a Banned Gaming App? What India's Law Actually Leaves Open

Published: 26 November 2026 · Last Reviewed: 26 November 2026 · By the All Yono India editorial team · How we check facts

Short answer: no law currently guarantees you a refund of money still sitting in a real-money gaming app's wallet. A provision that would have done exactly that — Rule 24 of the draft Online Gaming Rules — required platforms to refund outstanding balances, deposits, and winnings within 180 days of the ban taking effect, and explicitly said doing so wasn't "facilitating" a prohibited game. That provision was removed before the Rules were finally notified. What's left is Section 7 of the Act, which stops banks from processing gaming-related payments without saying whether returning an existing balance counts as one of those payments.

What the Draft Rules Actually Promised

When the Online Gaming Rules were first published in draft form on 2 October 2025, they included Rule 24: a requirement that platforms refund any outstanding user balance, deposit, or winning within 180 days of the prohibition commencing, and a statement that such a refund would not itself count as facilitating an online money game. Industry commentary at the time — including objections from the Internet and Mobile Association of India — argued the 180-day window was too short and didn't say what happens to balances nobody claims before it closes. Those objections were never addressed in a revision. Instead, when the Rules were finally notified, Rule 24 was gone entirely, with no public explanation for the removal.

Why That Removal Matters More Than It Sounds

Dropping Rule 24 didn't just remove a deadline — it removed the one sentence that would have told a bank it was safe to process a refund. Section 7 of the Act bars banks, financial institutions, and anyone else facilitating financial transactions from engaging in or facilitating a payment connected to an online money gaming service. Rule 24 would have carved refunds out of that prohibition. Without it, legal commentators have flagged, a platform or a bank processing a payout from an old gaming account has no explicit assurance that doing so isn't the same facilitation Section 7 forbids. That's a gap in the rules, not a confirmed ban on refunds either way — nobody has published a government clarification resolving it as of this writing.

What Actually Happened to Users' Balances So Far

Some users recovered their money before this gap became relevant. Through August 2025, as several large real-money platforms wound down cash-based formats ahead of the Act, reporting at the time described banks continuing to accept withdrawals from those platforms while blocking new deposits — a transition window, not a rule written into the Act itself. Companies made their own statements to users during that period about balances being safe to withdraw; those were business decisions, not legal guarantees, and a platform saying so in 2025 says nothing about a different platform's position today. What's left in limbo, per the legal analysis above, splits into two groups: balances already frozen as part of a separate enforcement action against a platform (which follows that action's own process, not an ordinary withdrawal request), and balances nobody came back to claim.

Where a Complaint Does, and Doesn't, Help

The Rules set up a three-tier grievance structure: a platform must run its own internal complaints process (Rule 20), an unsatisfied user can escalate to the Online Gaming Authority of India within 30 days of the platform's decision (with OGAI aiming, not guaranteeing, to resolve it within another 30 days), and a further appeal goes to the Secretary, Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology. That structure exists for platforms still operating under the Act's regulatory framework — it is not confirmed, in anything reviewed for this article, as a channel specifically built for recovering a balance from a platform whose money-gaming service is now prohibited outright. Treat it as the formal complaints route that exists, not as a confirmed refund mechanism.

What This Means If You Still Have a Balance Somewhere

  • There is currently no provision in the final Rules that requires a platform to refund your balance on a fixed timeline — the draft version that would have done this (Rule 24) was removed before notification.
  • A platform's own statement that "your balance is safe to withdraw" is that platform's position, not a legal guarantee, and it can change.
  • If a bank refuses to process an old withdrawal, that may reflect the same Section 7 ambiguity described above, not necessarily a mistake on the bank's side.
  • Money frozen as part of a specific enforcement action against a platform follows that action's separate legal process, not a normal withdrawal request.
  • If you believe you've been defrauded (asked to pay a "release fee," told to send money to a personal UPI ID to "unlock" a withdrawal, and so on), that's a cybercrime matter: call the National Cyber Crime Helpline on 1930 or report it at cybercrime.gov.in.

FAQs

Can I still withdraw money that was in a gaming app's wallet before the ban?

It depends on the platform and the bank processing the payout, not on a guarantee in the Act. Some platforms kept withdrawals open while blocking new deposits during 2025; whether that's still true for any given app today isn't something this article can confirm.

Does the Online Gaming Act require platforms to refund stuck balances?

Not in the final Rules. A draft provision (Rule 24) would have required a refund within 180 days and said doing so wasn't a prohibited payment — but it was removed before the Rules were notified, and nothing has replaced it.

Why would a bank refuse to process a withdrawal from an old gaming account?

Section 7 stops banks from facilitating payments connected to online money gaming, and without the refund exception Rule 24 would have provided, a bank may treat returning an old balance as the same prohibited facilitation. That's a legal ambiguity flagged by commentators, not a confirmed rule in either direction.

What happens to money frozen by an enforcement action against a platform?

That follows the separate legal process tied to that specific action, not an ordinary withdrawal request, and it's outside what this article covers.

What You Can Actually Check Right Now

If you have money in a real-money gaming account, the two concrete things to check are the platform's own current notice about withdrawals (not a 2025 statement) and whether your bank gives a specific reason for declining a payout. Neither the Act nor the Rules currently guarantee you a result either way — that gap is the point of this article, not a reason to assume the worst or the best.
