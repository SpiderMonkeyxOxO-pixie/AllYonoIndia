Does the Online Gaming Act Punish Players, or Only Operators?

Published: 27 November 2026 · Last Reviewed: 27 November 2026 · By the All Yono India editorial team · How we check facts

Short answer: the Act's long title states its purpose includes prohibiting "the offering, operation, facilitation, advertisement, promotion and participation in online money games" — but its only three operative prohibitions, in Chapter III, are Section 5 (offering), Section 6 (advertising), and Section 7 (payment facilitation by banks). Section 9, the penalty clause, only punishes contravention of Sections 5, 6, and 7: three years' imprisonment and a fine of up to ₹1 crore for offering, two years and up to ₹50 lakh for advertising, three years and up to ₹1 crore for payment facilitation, with harsher mandatory-minimum tiers on a repeat offence. No sub-clause of Section 9 reaches a player directly.

Sourcing note: the document directly read for this piece is the Bill as introduced in Lok Sabha on 20 August 2025 (Gazette of India, Extraordinary, Part II Section 2, available at egazette.gov.in) — not the final post-assent Act text. The official MeitY Act PDF and the India Code PDF both returned an error (HTTP 403) on a fresh fetch attempt made while writing this piece, so we could not re-verify the notified text directly today. This piece is based on a direct reading of the Bill as introduced, cross-checked against secondary legal commentary (SCC Online, Ikigai Law/Mondaq, PRS India, Bar & Bench) published after the Act's assent that corroborate the same penalty figures — not an independent re-reading of the final notified Act text.

What the Act's Long Title Promises vs What Chapter III Actually Prohibits

The long title of the Promotion and Regulation of Online Gaming Act, 2025 describes a sweeping purpose: to prohibit "the offering, operation, facilitation, advertisement, promotion and participation in online money games," among other things. Read on its own, that sentence puts a player's "participation" in the same list as an operator's "offering" and an advertiser's "promotion" — which is the line that fuels most of the confusion about whether playing itself is punishable. But a long title states purpose; it doesn't create an offence. The Act's operative prohibitions live in Chapter III, and there are exactly three of them: Section 5 bars offering an online money game, Section 6 bars advertising one, and Section 7 bars banks and other payment facilitators from processing a payment connected to one. "Participation" named in the long title has no matching operative section in Chapter III that mirrors it the way Sections 5, 6, and 7 mirror "offering," "advertisement," and "facilitation."

What Section 9 Actually Penalizes

Section 9 is the Act's penalty clause, and based on this reading of the Bill, it tracks Chapter III's three prohibitions one-for-one, not the long title's broader list. Contravening Section 5 by offering an online money game carries up to three years' imprisonment, a fine of up to ₹1 crore, or both. Contravening Section 6 by advertising one carries up to two years and a fine of up to ₹50 lakh. Contravening Section 7 by facilitating a payment for one carries up to three years and a fine of up to ₹1 crore. A second or subsequent conviction under the offering or payment-facilitation provisions steps up to a mandatory minimum term (reported in secondary commentary as three years, extendable to five) and a mandatory minimum fine (reported as ₹1 crore, extendable to ₹2 crore). Every one of those tiers is addressed to someone offering, advertising, or financially facilitating the game. None of them is addressed to the person playing it.

Could the Rules Have Added a Player Penalty?

A natural follow-up question is whether the 2026 Rules, made under the Act's rule-making power, quietly added a penalty for players that the Act itself doesn't contain. Based on the Bill text's own "Memorandum Regarding Delegated Legislation," that looks unlikely: the memorandum describes the rule-making power under Section 19 as covering only "procedure and administrative details" of a "normal character" — the kind of implementing detail delegated legislation is ordinarily limited to, not a vehicle for creating a new substantive criminal offence that Parliament didn't enact in the Act itself. That said, we have not independently re-confirmed this specific point against the final notified Rules text in this session, so treat it as a reading of the Bill's own stated intent for its delegated powers, not a line-by-line check of the Rules as published.

What This Doesn't Mean

None of the above is a reason to keep using a real-money gaming app. The underlying service remains fully prohibited regardless of whether a player faces direct criminal liability under Section 9. Offering a real-money game is illegal under Section 5. Advertising one is illegal under Section 6. Processing a payment connected to one is illegal under Section 7. Every one of those prohibitions is in force today, independent of this article's reading of who Section 9 penalizes. This piece answers a narrow legal question — whether Section 9 itself names a player-facing offence — and that answer is not a signal that the activity around it is safe, sanctioned, or free of other consequences.

What This Means If You're Using a Real-Money App Today

If you're currently using a real-money gaming app, the operator and any bank or payment processor still facilitating it are the parties exposed to the penalties described above. A player's own direct criminal exposure under Sections 5, 6, or 7 specifically is not established by the Act's own penalty clause, based on this reading of the Bill text. That is not the same as the activity being risk-free. Using a service that is prohibited at the operator and payment-processing level still means no consumer protection applies to you, and no law guarantees you'll be able to recover a balance left in the app — a gap covered in more detail in our stuck-balance explainer. Treat this article as answering one specific legal question, not as a reason to disregard those other risks.

FAQs

Does the Online Gaming Act punish players for playing, not just operators?

Based on a direct reading of the Bill as introduced, no player-facing penalty clause exists in Section 9; only offering, advertising, and payment facilitation are punished. This has not been independently re-verified against the final notified Act text.

Can I be arrested for playing a rummy app in India?

Not based on anything in Section 9 as read from the Bill text; the penalties there target operators, advertisers, and payment facilitators, not individual players, as far as this reading of the Bill goes.

Does this mean playing a real-money app is legal now?

No. The underlying service — offering, advertising, or facilitating payment for it — remains fully prohibited under the Act regardless of any player-penalty question. This is not a clearance to keep playing.

Could the Rules add a player penalty that the Act itself doesn't have?

The Act's own delegated-legislation memorandum describes its rule-making power as limited to procedural/administrative matters, suggesting the Rules likely couldn't add a substantive player offence — but this hasn't been independently re-confirmed against the final notified Rules text.

Where can I read the primary source myself?

The Bill as introduced is available via the Gazette of India at egazette.gov.in; the post-assent Act text is published by MeitY, though that specific link returned an error on our most recent check.
